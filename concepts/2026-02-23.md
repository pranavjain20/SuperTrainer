# Day 1 — Feb 23, 2026 (Phase 1b: Voice Pipeline Planning + Deepgram)

## Speech-to-Text (STT)

Speech-to-text converts audio into text. That's it — sound waves in, words out. Deepgram is the service we use. We send it audio bytes, it sends back a transcript with word-level timing and confidence scores.

Deepgram is the **ears**. It doesn't understand what was said — it just hears the sounds and writes them down. Understanding comes later (Claude).

## Keyterm Prompting

Deepgram has a feature where you can send it a "cheat sheet" of up to 100 words it should listen for. Without this, it might hear "RDL" and transcribe it as "are deal" or "Ardell." With keyterm prompting, we tell it: "these 100 terms exist in this domain — listen for them."

How we pick the 100 terms (priority order):
1. **Gym jargon** — RPE, AMRAP, superset, etc. (words Deepgram would never guess)
2. **Abbreviations** — RDL, OHP, GHR (short codes that sound like random syllables)
3. **Multi-word exercise names** — Romanian Deadlift, Bulgarian Split Squat (harder to recognize as a unit)

We skip common single words like "squat" or "bench" — Deepgram handles those fine on its own.

## One Data Source, Multiple Consumers

The exercise database (`exercise_db.json`) is one file with 142 exercises, but it feeds into multiple parts of the system:
- **Deepgram keyterms** — extract top 100 terms for speech recognition (Day 1)
- **Fuzzy matching** — match spoken exercise names to canonical names (Day 4)
- **Seed data** — populate the database for development/testing (Day 1)
- **The Brain** — domain knowledge for the conversational AI (Phase 3)

This is a pattern in software architecture: **single source of truth** with multiple downstream consumers. The exercise DB has no size constraint — only the keyterm list (one consumer) is limited to 100.
