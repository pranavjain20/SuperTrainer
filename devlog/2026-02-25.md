# Day 3 (Phase 1b) — Feb 25, 2026

## What Happened

Day 2 built the parser from scratch — two tools, ten rules, structured extraction. Day 3 extended it: what happens when the trainer isn't saying something new, but updating something they already said?

The answer required three things: giving the parser memory (session context), teaching it to classify intent (new vs modify vs observation), and having it output structured modifications. Built all three, 37 new tests, 433 total, all green.

## The Build

**Session context** — the parser now accepts prior entries formatted as numbered readable text. `[1] Exercise: bench press | Sets: 3x10 reps @ 80kg`. The numbered IDs become the targeting mechanism: when the trainer says "RPE 8 on those," Claude calls `modify_exercise_card` with `target_entry_id: 1`. Unambiguous.

Two design decisions mattered: context goes in the user message (not system prompt — that's for behavior rules, not per-request data), and the format is structured readable text (not JSON — too verbose, not narrative — no clear IDs to reference).

**Modify tool** — third tool added to the parser. `target_entry_id` for which entry, `action` ("add" vs "correct") for merge behavior, `target_sets` for targeting specific sets. The "add" vs "correct" distinction matters downstream: "also, RPE 8" layers new info on top, "no wait, 85 not 80" overwrites. Default is always "add" — correction requires explicit signal from the trainer. Safe by default.

**Five new prompt rules** — intent routing (check context before creating new), no modify without context, ambiguous references get yellow-flagged, modifications scoped to exercise cards only, "correct" is opt-in.

## The Product Catch

Caught something that would have been a real problem later. When the parser encounters ambiguity — say, two bench press entries and the trainer says "RPE 8 on bench" — the initial design created a yellow-flag observation card explaining the ambiguity. Technically correct. But observation cards are the trainer's words. Putting system-generated notices in that stream pollutes their data.

This came from thinking about it as a trainer, not as an engineer. Trainers aren't technical. They won't understand why there's a weird system message mixed in with their actual notes. And if the product shows them things that feel wrong or confusing, they lose trust. Once trust is gone, they stop using it. These are people who already have a workflow that works — pen and paper, a spreadsheet, whatever. The product has to be better than that, not weirder than that.

The fix: ambiguity flags don't become observation cards. The endpoint returns them separately as `clarifications_needed`, and the app shows them as dismissable prompts where the trainer picks the right exercise. Clean separation between trainer data and system concerns.
