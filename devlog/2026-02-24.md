# Day 2 (Phase 1b) — Feb 24, 2026

*The first real AI day. Built the Claude parser, learned tool_use end-to-end, and confronted the uncomfortable question of what my CS education means in a world where AI writes the code.*

## What happened today

Built `services/parser.py` — the component that takes a trainer's transcribed speech and turns it into structured exercise cards and observation cards using Claude's tool_use. This is the first real AI piece of SuperTrainer: not CRUD, not database schemas, but actually using a language model to understand natural language and extract structured data from it.

The code itself took maybe 2-3 hours. But this was a learning day by design, and most of the time was spent understanding *how* and *why* things work — not just getting them to work.

### How we built it

The first attempt went sideways. Claude Code wrote the entire parser in one shot — 250 lines of code, explained after the fact. I stopped it: "Why are you building without me reading, understanding, and asking questions?" Restarted. Claude explained after writing everything. Stopped it again: "I thought we'd do this incrementally together."

Third time was the charm. The pattern that worked: explain one concept, write that piece of code, I ask questions about it, then move to the next piece. Tool schema first, then system prompt, then dataclasses, then the parsing functions, then tests. Each piece small enough to understand fully before moving on.

It still feels like Claude is building and I'm watching — but I think that might be the right dynamic for now. What matters more than writing every line is understanding *what's being built and why*. The shape and purpose of each decision. I can explain tool_use to someone now. I couldn't yesterday.

### What we built

Two tool schemas — `record_exercise_card` and `record_observation_card`. The key insight: the tool name IS the classification. When Claude picks which tool to call, it's simultaneously deciding "is this an exercise or an observation?" No separate classification step needed.

A 10-rule system prompt that tells Claude how to behave: preserve exercise names exactly as spoken (no normalizing — that's Day 4), expand "3 sets of 10" into individual set records, don't invent data that wasn't in the transcript, flag ambiguity with a yellow observation card instead of guessing.

Frozen dataclasses for the output — `ParsedSet`, `ParsedExerciseCard`, `ParsedObservationCard`, `ParserResult`. Immutable because once Claude extracts the data, our code shouldn't accidentally modify it before it hits the database.

30 tests, all mocked. The mocking pattern is the same one from Day 1 (Deepgram): fake the external API, test that *our code* handles different response shapes correctly. We're not testing whether Claude parses well — that's Day 6. We're testing whether our code correctly converts Claude's response format into our data structures.

396 total tests passing. parser.py is ~375 lines.

### The product insight

Had a real-world insight from my own experience with my trainer. He has clients in different countries — some think in kg, some in lbs. When he writes notes by hand, he just writes whatever unit the client uses. We need the same: a per-client weight unit preference. When the parser doesn't hear a unit (trainer just says "80"), the system should default to that client's preference instead of guessing. Added this to the Day 4 validation layer plan. Small detail, but it's the kind of thing that only comes from actually being in the domain.

## What clicked

The big breakthrough was breaking through the black box illusion. AI — Claude — is one brain. And all the things people talk about — tool use, RAG, fine-tuning, embeddings — are just different ways of using that brain. I've been hearing these terms for months and they all jumbled together. "When do you use one vs another? How does this work vs that?" It was overwhelming because it felt like learning five different things simultaneously.

Today was step one in distilling that. We mapped it out: system prompts are sticky notes on the desk (behavioral instructions). Tool use is giving Claude forms instead of blank paper (structured output). RAG is pulling files from a cabinet before asking a question (retrieved context). Fine-tuning is sending the model to specialized training (rarely needed). Embeddings aren't even Claude — they're a separate model that turns text into searchable numbers.

One brain, different interfaces. That framework makes everything less intimidating. Tool use isn't some separate technology I need to master — it's just telling Claude "fill in this form instead of writing me an essay." Once I saw it that way, the parser design decisions started making sense on their own.

If I had to explain tool_use right now: Claude is trained to answer in sentences. But for our parser, we don't want sentences — we want structured data. Tool_use lets you maneuver Claude to give answers in the exact format you need. It's not just skipping a layer we'd have to build ourselves (like regex parsing) — it's using Claude's intelligence to make that layer smarter. A hand-built parser would be dumber AND slower.

## Reflections

### The speed vs depth tension

This is the thing I keep coming back to. If I was working on this alone, I'd spend three weeks on one feature. I'd make wrong design decisions, wrong technical decisions, debug them, and learn deeply from the struggle. Each mistake would burn something into my brain that stays forever.

With AI, it all happens in a few days instead of weeks or months. The understanding feels different — faster, but I'm not sure it's as deep.

And this leads to an uncomfortable question about my CS education. The world is moving to a place where AI handles the coding. So what was the point of four years of computer science? Am I just tuned to think a certain way — to know which questions to ask? Someone without CS wouldn't ask "why two tools instead of one?" — not because they're dumb, but because they don't even know that question exists. Is that all my education gave me? The ability to know which questions can be asked?

And then the deeper layer: is all this learning — tool_use, schema design, how Claude works under the hood — for my own intellectual development, or is it actually useful? Everything we're building together, all the architecture decisions, the insights from my own trainer experience, the schema design choices — is the human part actually irreplaceable, or is that getting automated too?

What helped was Claude's response: the questions I ask come from understanding that the question CAN be asked. That is the CS education. The coding itself is getting commoditized, but the architecture decisions, the "what needs to be built," the real-world insights — every single one of those is going into the product and can't be automated. The system we're building is shaped by my real understanding. Per-client weight units came from my experience with my trainer. Two tools instead of one came from me asking "why?" The parser schema exists because I spent 10 days building the data model it maps to. None of that is automatable.

And there's another piece that matters: we haven't reached the testing and breaking phase yet. The real deep understanding — the bone-deep kind that comes from struggle — that happens when you use the thing, it breaks in unexpected ways, and you figure out why. Day 6 is when the parser meets real trainer speech and fails. Debugging those failures is where the understanding becomes permanent. The struggle isn't gone. It's just ahead of me, not behind me.

It's a weird phase to be in. Coming to terms with what matters in a world where the mechanics of coding are being commoditized, while simultaneously building something that clearly requires human judgment and domain insight at every step. I don't have a clean answer yet. But I'm more at peace with it than I was yesterday.

## What's next

Day 3 adds context: the phone sends previous entries alongside each new clip, so Claude can resolve "same weight," "second set," and "actually that was RPE 8" by looking at what came before.
